How a law is made

  1. 1ProposalSubmitted by the government, an MP or a group of MPs, the Senate or a regional assembly.
  2. 21st readingA debate on the purpose of the bill. The Chamber rejects it, returns it for redrafting, or sends it to committees. In exceptional cases it can pass it straight away.at the earliest 10 days after distribution
  3. 3CommitteesThe lead committee discusses the bill in detail and recommends passing it, not passing it, or passing it with changes.usually 60 days
  4. 42nd readingGeneral and detailed debate. In the detailed part MPs table amendments.
  5. 53rd readingVotes on a possible rejection, on the amendments and finally on the bill as a whole.at the earliest 14 days after the amendments are distributed
  6. 6SenatePasses the law, rejects it, returns it with amendments, or decides not to deal with it. The Senate does not decide on the state budget.30 days
  7. 7PresidentSigns the law, or returns it to the Chamber.15 days
  8. 8Collection of LawsOnce promulgated, the law is valid. It usually takes effect on 1 January or 1 July.
When it goes differently Passing in the 1st reading. Only if the sponsor proposed it together with the bill and two clubs or 50 MPs do not object by the end of the general debate. Not possible for constitutional laws, the state budget and treaties transferring powers to an international organisation. Amendments cannot be tabled; only errors and the effective date may be corrected. State of legislative emergency. Declared by the Speaker at the government's request when people's rights or state security are seriously threatened or major economic damage is imminent. At the government's request the Speaker can then decide on a shortened procedure: a government bill skips the 1st reading, speeches can be cut down to 5 minutes (except speakers for clubs and speakers with priority) and the 3rd reading can follow straight after the 2nd. The state budget has its own rules, see below.

Who can propose a law

The governmentAn MP or a group of MPsThe SenateA regional assembly

A bill is always submitted to the Chamber of Deputies. Only the government may submit the state budget and the state closing account. The government has 30 days to give its opinion on bills it did not submit. If it does not respond, it is deemed to agree. Its opinion does not bind the Chamber.

A government bill is presented in the Chamber by an appointed member of the government, a bill of a group of MPs by one of them. On this site we call them the sponsor.

Committees

A committee is a smaller group of MPs that examines a bill in more detail than the whole Chamber. The Chamber has committees by area, for example budget, agriculture, or media and culture.

The lead committee is chosen by the Chamber at the end of the 1st reading and is in charge of the bill until the 3rd reading. It recommends whether to pass the bill and can propose changes. After the 2nd reading it usually gives its view on all amendments and proposes the order in which they will be voted on. Other committees may also comment on the bill.

A committee's conclusion is a resolution, which in practice is published as a Chamber print with a number after the slash. The rapporteur is the MP who informs the Chamber about the bill and the committee's discussion and announces the voting order in the 3rd reading.

Amendments

An amendment changes, adds to or deletes part of the bill under discussion.

  • Who and when: an MP in a committee or in the detailed debate of the 2nd reading. The amendment is usually published electronically beforehand and referred to in the debate. A committee tables its changes in a resolution.
  • Summary: MPs receive all tabled amendments in one document. In practice the individual items are labelled with letters and numbers (A1, B2…).
  • Voting in the 3rd reading: the rapporteur announces the voting order. Before each vote the rapporteur and the sponsor usually say whether they recommend the amendment. If passing one amendment rules out another, the other is no longer voted on.
  • On this site we count amendments by the individual items that are voted on, and show whether they were tabled by MPs of the governing parties, the opposition, both together, or a committee.

The Senate and the President

The Senate…What follows
passes the law, does not respond within 30 days, or resolves not to deal with itThe law goes to the President for signature. The Senate must always vote on a constitutional law.
rejects itThe Chamber can still pass the law, but needs 101 votes. A constitutional law fails if the Senate rejects it.
returns it with amendmentsThe Chamber votes on the Senate's version; a majority of those present is enough. If it rejects it, it can pass its original version, but needs 101 votes. No new changes can be added.

The President has 15 days. He signs the law, or returns it to the Chamber with reasons. A returned law is valid only if 101 MPs vote for it again. The President cannot return a constitutional law. If he does not return the law in time, he should sign it; according to the Constitutional Court, however, a missing signature does not prevent the law from being promulgated.

A law is signed by the Speaker of the Chamber, the President and the Prime Minister. It is valid from its promulgation in the electronic Collection of Laws (e-Sbírka). It usually takes effect, that is, becomes binding, on 1 January or 1 July. Another day is possible only in exceptional cases when urgent public interest requires it.

How many votes are needed

DecisionVotes in favour needed
For the Chamber to be able to vote at allat least a third of MPs present (67)
Ordinary law, most resolutionsmore than half of the MPs present
Constitutional law, treaties transferring powers to an international organisation3/5 of all MPs (120)
Overriding the Senate or the President, no confidence in the governmentmore than half of all MPs (101)

What counts is the number of votes in favour. Every MP who is logged in counts as present. With a majority of those present, abstaining or not voting therefore has the same effect on the result as voting against. With a majority of all MPs (101, 120) it is the same as being absent.

What else the Chamber deals with

State budget

Submitted only by the government and approved only by the Chamber; the Senate does not vote on it. The 1st reading approves the basic figures: revenue, expenditure, the deficit and how it is covered, the relation to regional and municipal budgets and the scope of authorisations. These cannot be changed afterwards; MPs can only move money within the budget.

International treaties

Important treaties, for example on people's rights and obligations, on membership of international organisations, or political and economic treaties, need the consent of both the Chamber and the Senate. The President then ratifies them.

Reports

The Chamber discusses reports of institutions, for example the annual reports of Czech Television and Czech Radio or the National Budget Council's report on the long-term sustainability of public finances. It takes note of them, approves them, or does not approve them. What follows is set by the law on the institution concerned.

Interpellations

An MP's question to the government or a minister. A written interpellation must be answered within 30 days. Oral interpellations take place on Thursday afternoons. If an MP is not satisfied with a written answer, the Chamber discusses it and can express disagreement with it. The minister must then answer again within 30 days.

Voting terms

Chamber print (sněmovní tisk)
A numbered written document for the Chamber's proceedings in a given electoral period. Only some prints are bills; others are, for example, committee resolutions, reports, international treaties or written interpellations the Chamber discusses.
Sponsor
The author of the bill. A government bill is defended in the Chamber by an appointed member of the government.
Rapporteur
The MP who guides the Chamber through the bill, summarises the committee's work and announces the voting order in the 3rd reading.
Procedural vote
A vote on how the sitting proceeds, not on the content of the law. For example on the agenda, the length of speeches, a break or an objection.
Excused, not logged in, abstained
An excused MP reported their absence; psp.cz sometimes adds excuses with a delay. An MP not logged in was not logged in with their card during the vote. An MP who abstained was logged in but voted neither for nor against.
Challenged vote
An objection to the course or result of a vote, for example when the device recorded a different vote for an MP. The Chamber votes on it without debate. If it upholds it, the vote is repeated and the new result applies.
Void vote
A vote declared void, usually because of a technical error. Its result is disregarded and it is usually repeated.
Objection to the chair's conduct
An MP disagrees with how the chair is running the sitting. The Chamber decides on it by a vote.
Revocation
Cancelling an earlier decision. For amendments and some other motions only an MP who voted for them may propose it. A rejection of a bill cannot be revoked.
Fixed voting time
The Chamber sets when a bill will be voted on. At that moment the debate ends even if more speakers are signed up. Only in entirely exceptional cases, and when MPs have had enough time to study and comment on the matter.
Limit on speaking time
The Chamber can shorten speeches, to no less than 10 minutes, and limit the number of contributions to two. This also applies to the rapporteur, a speaker for a club and speakers with priority; their first speech in the debate may last at most 30 minutes.
Priority right to speak
The right to take the floor at any time. It belongs to the President, members of the government, the Chamber's leadership, club chairs and chairs of parties that have a club formed at the start of the electoral period.
Factual remark
A short reaction, at most 2 minutes, to another speaker's contribution on the matter under discussion.
Parliamentary club
MPs elected for the same party who act together in the Chamber. At the start of the electoral period a club needs at least 3 members, a new club formed during the period at least 10.
